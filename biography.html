01 . PERSONAL STORY

BIOGRAPHY OF MARIE CURIE

Marie Curie, born Maria Salomea Skłodowska on 7 November 1867 in Warsaw, Poland, was one of the most influential scientists in history. She was a brilliant physicist and chemist whose remarkable discoveries changed the world of science forever. From an early age, Marie showed a deep interest in education, mathematics, and scientific research. She was born into a family of teachers who encouraged learning and knowledge. However, growing up in Poland during a time of political difficulties and limited educational opportunities for women was not easy. Despite these challenges, she remained determined to achieve her dreams and never allowed difficulties to stop her from pursuing education. In 1891, Marie moved to Paris, France, to continue her higher education at the University of Paris, also known as the Sorbonne. She studied physics and mathematics and worked extremely hard to achieve academic success. Her life in Paris was financially difficult, but her passion for science kept her focused on her goals. During this period, she met Pierre Curie, a talented physicist who shared her interest in scientific research. They married in 1895 and became one of the most remarkable scientific partnerships in history. Together, they began conducting experiments that would eventually lead to discoveries that transformed modern science. Marie Curie became particularly interested in the study of radiation and began investigating the properties of uranium. Through her careful experiments, she discovered that radiation originated from within atoms. She introduced the term radioactivity to describe this phenomenon. Her research led to one of the greatest scientific breakthroughs of the nineteenth century. In 1898, Marie and Pierre Curie discovered two new chemical elements, Polonium and Radium. Marie named Polonium after her homeland, Poland, while Radium was named because of its radioactive properties. These discoveries greatly expanded scientific understanding and opened new possibilities for research in physics and chemistry. Marie Curie's extraordinary achievements soon brought her international recognition. In 1903, she received the Nobel Prize in Physics, which she shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel for their research on radiation. She became the first woman to receive a Nobel Prize, making history in the scientific world. Sadly, Pierre Curie died in 1906, but Marie continued their research with great determination. She later became the first woman to serve as a professor at the University of Paris. In 1911, she received her second Nobel Prize, this time in Chemistry, for her discoveries and research on Radium and Polonium. She became the first person in history to win Nobel Prize.

Albert Einstein

Albert Einstein was one of the greatest scientists in human history, widely recognized for his revolutionary contributions to physics. He was born on March 14, 1879, in Ulm, Germany. From an early age, Einstein showed a strong interest in mathematics and science and developed a natural curiosity about how the universe worked. He studied physics and mathematics at the Swiss Federal Polytechnic in Zurich and later became a teacher and scientist. His groundbreaking theory of relativity completely transformed the understanding of space, time, gravity and energy. His famous equation, E = mc², demonstrated the relationship between mass and energy and became one of the most recognized equations in science. In 1921, Einstein received the Nobel Prize in Physics for his explanation of the photoelectric effect, which played an important role in the development of quantum physics. Throughout his life, he worked at several prestigious institutions and became an influential voice in scientific and humanitarian discussions. He later moved to the United States, where he continued his scientific work at Princeton University. Albert Einstein passed away on April 18, 1955, but his discoveries continue to influence modern science, technology and our understanding of the universe.

Mother Teresa

Mother Teresa was a globally recognized humanitarian and religious figure who devoted her life to helping poor, sick and disadvantaged people. She was born as Agnes Gonxha Bojaxhiu on August 26, 1910, in Skopje, which is now the capital of North Macedonia. From a young age, she developed a strong interest in religious service and felt inspired to dedicate her life to helping people in need. At the age of eighteen, she left her home to join a religious community in Ireland before travelling to India, where she began working as a teacher in Calcutta, now known as Kolkata. During her time in India, she became deeply concerned about the difficult conditions faced by poor and vulnerable communities. In 1948, she decided to leave her teaching position and dedicate herself to serving people living in poverty. In 1950, she founded the Missionaries of Charity, an organization established to provide care and support to people who were often neglected by society. Under her leadership, the organization expanded its work and established homes, shelters and charitable centres in different parts of the world. Her efforts focused on providing assistance to people experiencing poverty, illness, homelessness and social difficulties. Through her humanitarian activities, Mother Teresa became internationally known as a symbol of compassion, service and dedication. In 1979, she received the Nobel Peace Prize for her work in bringing help to suffering humanity. She continued her charitable activities until her health began to decline in her later years. Mother Teresa passed away on September 5, 1997, in Kolkata, India. In 2016, she was officially declared a saint by the Catholic Church. Her life remains widely remembered for her commitment to humanitarian service and her efforts to help vulnerable people.

Dr. Abdus Salam

Dr. Abdus Salam was one of the most brilliant physicists in the history of Pakistan and an internationally respected scientist whose discoveries made a significant contribution to modern physics. He was born on January 29, 1926, in Jhang, Punjab, British India, now part of Pakistan. From an early age, he demonstrated extraordinary academic abilities and developed a deep interest in mathematics and science. He received his early education in Pakistan and later travelled to England to study at the University of Cambridge, where he earned degrees in mathematics and physics and completed his doctorate in theoretical physics. Salam became famous for his groundbreaking research on the unification of two fundamental forces of nature, the electromagnetic force and the weak nuclear force. His work helped scientists develop a deeper understanding of the basic structure of matter and the interactions between elementary particles. In 1951, he returned to Pakistan and worked as a professor, sharing his knowledge with students and encouraging scientific education. He later became a professor at Imperial College London and founded the International Centre for Theoretical Physics in Italy to support scientists from developing countries. In 1979, he became the first Pakistani scientist to receive the Nobel Prize in Physics, sharing the award with Sheldon Glashow and Steven Weinberg. His achievement brought international recognition to Pakistan and demonstrated the importance of education, research and scientific dedication. Abdus Salam passed away on November 21, 1996, but his contributions continue to influence scientists and inspire young students around the world.

Niels Bohr

Niels Bohr was a famous Danish physicist who made remarkable contributions to the understanding of atomic structure and the development of modern quantum physics. He was born on October 7, 1885, in Copenhagen, Denmark, into an educated family that encouraged learning and scientific curiosity. His father was a professor of physiology, which helped create an environment where scientific discussions and intellectual exploration were common. Bohr developed an interest in physics during his school years and later studied at the University of Copenhagen, where he earned his doctorate in physics in 1911. He continued his research in England, working with leading scientists and developing new ideas about the structure of atoms. In 1913, he introduced the Bohr atomic model, which explained how electrons occupy specific energy levels around an atom's nucleus and how they release or absorb energy when moving between these levels. This model became an important step in the development of atomic physics and helped scientists understand the behaviour of matter at a microscopic level. His research transformed the scientific understanding of atoms and influenced many future discoveries in physics and chemistry. In 1922, Niels Bohr received the Nobel Prize in Physics for his investigations into the structure of atoms and the radiation they emit. He also established an important research centre in Copenhagen, which later became known as the Niels Bohr Institute and attracted scientists from different parts of the world. Throughout his career, he encouraged international scientific cooperation and supported the peaceful use of scientific discoveries. Niels Bohr passed away on November 18, 1962, leaving behind a scientific legacy that continues to shape modern physics.

Nelson Mandela

Nelson Mandela was a remarkable South African leader who dedicated his life to fighting racial discrimination and promoting equality, justice and freedom. He was born on July 18, 1918, in the village of Mvezo, South Africa. During his early life, he witnessed the unfair treatment of Black South Africans under a system known as apartheid, which separated people according to race and denied the majority population equal rights. Mandela studied law and became involved in the struggle against racial discrimination. His determination to achieve freedom and equal opportunities made him one of the most important leaders in South African history. Because of his political activities and opposition to apartheid, he spent 27 years in prison. Despite these difficult years, he remained committed to his vision of a peaceful and democratic country. In 1990, he was released from prison and continued working towards national reconciliation. In 1993, Nelson Mandela received the Nobel Peace Prize jointly with F. W. de Klerk for their efforts to peacefully end apartheid and establish democracy in South Africa. In 1994, he became South Africa's first Black president, making history as a symbol of freedom and unity. He passed away on December 5, 2013, but his courage, leadership and dedication to equality continue to inspire people across the world.

Alexander Fleming

Alexander Fleming was a distinguished Scottish scientist and bacteriologist who changed the history of medicine through his discovery of penicillin, the first widely used antibiotic. He was born on August 6, 1881, in Ayrshire, Scotland, and developed an interest in learning and observation during his childhood. Before beginning his medical education, he worked in London for a short period before receiving an opportunity to study medicine at St Mary's Hospital Medical School. After completing his studies, he became interested in bacteriology and dedicated his career to understanding microorganisms and discovering ways to fight bacterial infections. During his research, Fleming carefully studied the behaviour of bacteria and different substances that could prevent their growth. In 1928, while working in his laboratory, he noticed that a particular type of mould had stopped the growth of bacteria around it. He investigated this unusual observation and identified the antibacterial substance produced by the mould, which he named penicillin. This discovery eventually transformed medical treatment and provided doctors with a powerful way to fight many dangerous bacterial infections. Although Fleming discovered penicillin, its development into a widely available medicine required further research by scientists Howard Florey and Ernst Boris Chain. Their combined efforts helped make penicillin available for medical use and saved millions of lives. In 1945, Fleming shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Florey and Chain for their discovery and development of penicillin. His work marked a major turning point in medical history and opened new possibilities for treating infectious diseases. Alexander Fleming passed away on March 11, 1955, but his discovery remains one of the most important achievements in modern medicine.
